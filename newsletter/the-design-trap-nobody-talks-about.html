The Creative Shift June 2, 2026

The Design Trap Nobody Talks About

Jeff Schenck · Chief Brand Strategist & Fractional CMO

Most brand builds start with strategy and end with a developer waiting on a file that doesn't exist yet.

That gap has killed more good work than bad briefs ever will.

THE SETUP

A client came to me needing a full website rebuild. Not a cosmetic refresh. A ground-up repositioning with behaviorally-worded copy, persona-mapped color psychology, and a design system built around how their buyers actually make decisions.

Before a single pixel was placed, I started where I always start: behavior.

Who is this person? What do they notice first? What makes them stay? What makes them leave without saying a word?

I pulled persona data. Mapped the emotional arc of the buyer journey. Chose typography that signals trust without saying "trustworthy." Chose a color palette not because it looked good in a deck, but because research on that specific buyer profile told me exactly how color temperature affects purchase confidence in that category.

This is what most agencies skip. They pick what looks nice. I build what converts.

WHERE IT WENT SIDEWAYS

Here is where I made a completely human mistake.

I should have pushed for a different platform to build as they had Canva and a hosted web prototype. Not Figma. I know Figma. I teach Figma-adjacent thinking. But I had let my subscription lapse and didn't want to spin it back up for a single engagement.

So I designed somewhere faster.

The work was right. The design was solid. The behavioral architecture was exactly where it needed to be. And then I handed it to the developer and hit the oldest wall in brand work: the file the developer needed did not exist in the format they could use.

No exportable component structure. No handoff-ready specs. Just a designed URL and a Canva file that a developer was not going to build from.

The project stalled.

THE PIVOT THAT CHANGED HOW I WORK

Instead of going backward, I went forward.

I gave Claude the live URL of the hosted prototype. I told it what the site was for, what behavioral intent was behind each section, what the buyer psychology required in terms of hierarchy and copy framing. And I asked it to rebuild the site in clean, deployable code based on what it saw.

It worked.

Not just technically. It understood the layout intent. It preserved the section hierarchy. It translated the behavioral structure into code that a developer could take and run with on day one.

What could have been a two-week delay was resolved in an afternoon.

THE DESIGN-TO-BUILD TRANSFER PROBLEM

This is not an unusual story. Most brand strategists and creative directors have a version of it.

You design in one environment. The developer lives in another. The handoff breaks in the middle.

The traditional fix is always: design in the right tool from the start.

That is still true. Figma is still the right answer when you have the setup ready. But the reality of fast-moving client work is that decisions get made under constraint. Timelines compress. Tools you planned to use are not available. You build where you can.

What changed for me is the understanding that the final design artifact no longer has to be a perfectly formatted file. If the design exists, rendered and live somewhere, AI can bridge that gap.

This is what I am now calling The URL-to-Handoff Method.

THE URL-TO-HANDOFF METHOD

This is the framework. Three steps.

Step 1: Build behavioral intent first. Before you touch any design tool, document the persona, the emotional trigger points, the color psychology, and the copy hierarchy. This is the brief the AI will need to understand what it is rebuilding and why. A design without behavioral intent is just decoration.

Step 2: Build the design wherever you can execute fastest. Figma is ideal. Canva works. A hosted web prototype works. Webflow works. What matters is that the design exists in a live, rendered state that a browser can read and an AI can interpret. Done beats perfect in the handoff moment.

Step 3: Feed the URL, not the file. Give Claude the live URL. Give it the behavioral brief. Tell it the intent behind each section. Ask it to translate the design into production-ready code. The output will not be a generic template. It will reflect the actual architecture you built because it is reading what you built.

The developer gets code. The design intent survives. The timeline holds.

WHAT THIS MEANS STRATEGICALLY

The implication here is bigger than one project recovery.

For years, the design-to-development handoff has been the single most expensive friction point in brand and web production. Hours billed, timelines missed, and design intent diluted because the file format, the tool, or the communication chain broke down somewhere in the middle.

AI just changed the equation.

You no longer need a perfect file to deliver a precise outcome. You need a live design and a documented behavioral intent. That combination is enough to bridge the gap.

This does not replace Figma. It does not replace developers. It changes the recovery cost when the process breaks, and it changes the speed ceiling for strategists who work at the pace of actual client demand.

ONE FOR THE ROAD

The design file is not the work. The behavioral intent behind it is. When you can transfer that intent, you can transfer anything.
